Speaking to devices directly

A FIWARE deployment normally has two tiers: devices speak their own protocols to an IoT Agent, the agent translates to NGSI-LD and forwards to a context broker. coraine can do the device side itself, through its bridge plugins - which makes the tiers a build and configuration choice, not an architecture.

Built: the bridges this rests on - DDS and ROS 2, MQTT topics, Modbus TCP registers. Not built: the cor-agent edge build, released bridges for the other device protocols (LWM2M/CoAP, OPC-UA, LoRaWAN), and a registration whose endpoint names a bridge (a value read on demand). Tracked in the roadmap. Prototype bridges for each FIWARE IoT Agent's protocol are listed in FIWARE IoT Agents.

A deployment that starts with one broker and grows splits the tiers by changing how it is built and where the pieces run - not by changing products. cor-agent is the name of the first shape's edge build: this broker, compiled down by its per-feature switches and shipped as its own image - a build configuration, not a separate codebase.

A device is a foreign endpoint

A DDS topic carrying a robot's pose and an MQTT topic carrying a thermostat's temperature are the same problem: something that does not speak NGSI-LD holds a value that belongs in an attribute. Device protocols use the bridge model every bridge uses:

  • A bridge is a transport instance - the MQTT connection, the CoAP server, the DDS participant. Few, fixed at start-up.
  • A channel ties one foreign endpoint to one entity attribute, with a direction and a retention policy.

"North" and "south" say where a deployment puts things; they are not two mechanisms.

Flow or delegation

A device's value reaches the broker in one of two ways, and the specification names both - ETSI TS 104 175, Annex G, Suggested actuation workflows:

The value...Modelled asAnnex G's workflow
flows through the broker - pushed by the device, or pushed to ita channelsubscription / notification (§ G.4.2, § G.5)
lives at the device or its adapter, fetched when reada registration whose endpoint names a bridge schemeforwarding (§ G.4.3, § G.6)

Lazy attributes are why the second exists. A device's battery level is read when someone asks, because polling it drains what is measured: the broker holds nothing and fetches on demand. Over the push model the same read is a notification to the adapter, a write back and the client's GET waiting on both; forwarding is one hop. One protocol often wants both - OPC-UA monitored items push and its reads pull; LWM2M observes and reads - so the choice is per attribute, not per protocol.

Annex G is informative: a workflow, not a mechanism of the API. For commands that mechanism is Service Execution (doc/service-execution.md): a command is a service of the device's entity, and the bridge plugin its executor. Writing an attribute stays a state change that a channel carries.

Device provisioning - registering a device and mapping its readings to attributes, in bulk, from templates, with service groups and keys - is a layer over channels and Service Registrations: a device's measures become a DataChannel, each command a Service Registration (designs: IoT Agent measures, IoT Agents as Service Executors).

Transport and payload are two axes

FIWARE's agent library configures device communications "regardless of the payload, syntax or transport protocol used". Split the same way, the plugins are a sum, not a product:

TransportsPayload codecs
HTTP, MQTT, AMQP, CoAP, Kafka, DDSJSON, UltraLight 2.0, LWM2M objects, ISOXML/ADAPT, CSV

UltraLight over MQTT and UltraLight over HTTP are one codec and two transports, not two agents. LoRaWAN and Sigfox carry network semantics of their own, not just a payload, and stay whole.

What a broker at the edge adds

An IoT Agent is a protocol adapter: it provisions devices, forwards their measurements to a broker, and answers the broker as a registered context provider for lazy attributes and commands. It serves no subscriptions or registrations of its own, hosts no context, answers no queries and has no temporal API - it is paired with a broker to be useful. A broker doing the same job also brings:

  • Subscriptions at the edge - a rule reacting to a sensor runs where the sensor is, with no round trip, and keeps working while the uplink is down.
  • Context hosted locally - device state queried on the spot with the whole query language: q, geo-queries, attrs, projections.
  • Registrations both ways - the edge broker as a context source for a central one, or federated with peers.
  • Temporal history at the edge, through the same history plugins.
  • Snapshots, tenants, JSON-LD @context handling - at the point the data arrives.

The cost is size and operational surface: an agent is a smaller thing to run. The per-feature compilation is what keeps an edge build to what its deployment turned on.
